Storm deaths fell
per resident.

From the 1940s to 2020–25, reported tornado, flood, and hurricane deaths fell modestly in raw counts and much more per U.S. resident. The pattern is consistent with adaptation, but the series cannot isolate its causes or measure risk in a storm’s path.

The hazards differ

Flood deaths rose in the raw NWS counts from the 1940s to 2020–25, while tornado deaths fell. Hurricanes swing sharply with a few catastrophic years, including 2005. The combined trend hides these differences. 1

Population changes the comparison

Dividing the annual combined death count by the population of each year, then averaging within periods, gives a clearer national-scale trend than raw counts alone. It still does not hold storm frequency, location, building quality, warning systems, or reporting practices fixed. 1, 2

Flood exposure depends on the unit of measurement

A Census working paper examining U.S. moves from 1999–2023 finds population growth in blocks classified as flood-prone. At the parcel level, however, inflows and outflows from mapped flood exposure approximately balance; the share of households in exposed parcels stays near 14.2–14.4%. Block-level growth does not establish a rising share of households on exposed parcels. 3

Three ways to count temperature deaths

Incident reports

NWS records heat fatalities in its hazard series; its average rises from 115 per year in the 2010s to 382 in 2020–25. Reporting and classification can change, so this is a descriptive comparison. 1

Excess deaths

For the 1995 Chicago heat wave, a CDC report gives 485 heat-related deaths and 739 excess deaths. These are different definitions applied to the same event. 4

Statistical attribution

Burke and coauthors estimate that ambient temperature accounts for 5–12% of deaths in the countries studied, with cold accounting for more than heat. Their modeled estimate is not an NWS incident count or an estimate of deaths caused by anthropogenic warming. 5

The economics of adaptation

Better warnings, buildings, forecasting, and response capacity may help explain the decline. This series cannot isolate their effects. Additional protection should be weighed against its full cost and feasible alternatives.

Reliable, affordable electricity, resilient infrastructure, and smarter siting can reduce harm without assuming that every place faces the same risk. Where public spending or insurance rules shape decisions, the practical question is who receives the benefit, who pays, and whether the next dollar prevents enough expected damage to justify it.

A falling national death rate does not show that hazards are weakening. Population is only a rough denominator; local exposure and costs matter for decisions.

Methods & sources

I transcribed annual tornado, flood, and hurricane fatalities from the NWS 81-year U.S. hazard summary (1940–2025). I combined the three categories by year. For each year, I divided that count by the Census July 1 U.S. population estimate and multiplied by one million. The figures above are unweighted averages of annual values within each period; 2020–25 is a six-year period. I kept heat separate because its measurement raises a different question.

The historical NWS reporting area and the Census population series do not match perfectly: NWS reporting can include U.S. territories, while the denominator primarily covers the 50 states and D.C. Historical population estimates and reporting also differ across time. The rate should be read as an approximate descriptive index. It does not establish causation or adjust for where storms struck.
