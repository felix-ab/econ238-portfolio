The arithmetic

Shift = beef cut × share of Americans taking part.

Beef land left = 3,256,000 km² × (1 − shift). Eshel et al. (2014) put all US animal-product land at about 3.7 million km², 88% of it for beef: pasture 79 points, hay and silage 7, grain 2. Pasture is therefore 89.8% of beef land.

Replacement land = 3,256,000 km² × shift × (land for one replacement plate ÷ land for one beef serving). Land per serving = protein in the serving × Poore & Nemecek (2018) m² per 100 g protein: beef herd 163.6, chicken 7.06, eggs 5.65, other pulses 7.27, tofu 2.20 (global means).

Plate size. By appetite: same 113 g as the beef, because people tend to eat a steady weight of food rather than steady calories (Bell, Rolls et al. 1998). For muscle: enough of each food to reach the leucine target. Nutrients for every food come from USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy, cooked forms).

Map. Pasture is spread over US half-degree cells in proportion to cattle head (FAO Gridded Livestock of the World, 2010). Feed land follows maize area, tofu land follows soybean area, bean and lentil land follows pulse area, and chicken and egg land follows maize + soy area (MapSPAM 2010, physical area). Dot area = km² × pixels per km² of the equal-area projection.

04 / WHAT THE MODEL SHOWS

Land isn’t the hard part.
Leucine is.

Leucine is the essential amino acid that switches on muscle protein synthesis (through the mTOR pathway). There is no hypertrophy without enough of it at a meal, and the other eight essential amino acids have to be there too.

01

Every swap frees most of the land.

Beef needs about 164 m² of land per 100 g of protein; chicken needs 7 and tofu 2. Even a plant plate sized to hit the leucine target uses roughly 4% of the land of the beef it replaces. That result holds across every option here.

02

Eating by appetite misses the target.

A beef-sized plate of beans or lentils carries about 0.75 g of leucine against a 2.5 g target, plus 8–9 g of fiber that fills you up first. Most eaters swapping beef without a plan get less protein and less leucine.

03

Eating for muscle costs plate and calories.

Reaching 2.5 g of leucine takes about 350 g of beans (over 450 kcal) or 380 g of lentils, against 289 kcal for the beef. After 50 the target rises to about 3 g, so the plant plate gets bigger still. Chicken hits the target in less than the beef’s weight.

04

Some nutrients don’t come back.

Plants carry no B12 (the only form in which people use cobalt) and less, worse-absorbed zinc and iron. Tofu and legumes do beat beef on copper. Choose “Pregnant” or “Adult, 71+” to see how the gaps shift.

Why processing plants are left out

Slaughterhouses, packing plants and distribution take very little land next to 3.3 million km² of pasture and feed, and replacement foods need processing too. Counting them for beef alone would bias the comparison. The model keeps the farm-land boundary for every food.

What the map can and can’t claim

The totals are national. The map distributes them by where cattle and crops are today, which is an allocation, not a survey of individual ranches. Cattle counts include dairy and feedlot animals, so some feedlot counties carry more pasture than they really have. Freed land is land no longer needed for food. Whether it is rewilded, reused or kept in production is a separate question.

Limits and next questions
  1. Eshel’s land totals average 2000–2010. About 19% of US beef (carcass weight) is imported, and its land sits abroad.
  2. Poore & Nemecek’s beef mean is global. Their median is about half, so per-serving land ratios are rough, but beef stays more than 10× any alternative under either figure.
  3. Most western rangeland is arid and can’t grow crops. “Freed” acres are not all farmable acres.
  4. Leucine targets are per-meal thresholds from muscle protein synthesis studies. Whole-day diets, training and digestibility (the DIAAS score) also matter.
  5. Next: model whole days rather than single meals, add dairy-herd beef, and weight the appetite and muscle lanes with survey data.
