The proposition

Buy Time for Birds

The ballot question

Should a local conservation group organize a voluntary $2,500 fund for a 2027 pilot, paying one willing farmer up to $2,000 to delay mowing a suitable 20-acre nesting hayfield until July 15, with up to $500 for habitat checks and monitoring? Launch only with full funding, a suitable field, and a signed agreement; otherwise return contributions. A YES vote supports this proposal, not a donation or pledge.

What is the deal?

Buy a nesting window, not a farm. The pilot would contract for no mowing, grazing, or other field disturbance before July 15, 2027, on 20 contiguous acres with nesting Bobolinks. A willing farmer receives up to $100 per acre; donors supply 100 voluntary $25 contributions. The farmer gives up early cutting and accepts possible losses in hay quality or feed value. Bird populations and people who value them receive the intended benefit. A local conservation group would have to agree to administer the fund and recruit a qualified field assessor. No group, farmer, or donor has committed. The $2,500 budget is a proposal, not a price quote. There is no promised number of birds saved. Continued hay production after the nesting window helps maintain the grassland.

The best case for YES

The farmer controls the harvest date, but people elsewhere also value the birds. Asking the farmer to absorb the whole conservation cost makes delay less attractive. A voluntary contract lets those beneficiaries pay for a specific change while the farmer keeps ownership and can decline. Existing delayed-haying programs show that this kind of exchange can operate with today's tools. A one-season pilot limits the commitment. Vetting a field with nesting birds and paying only for a change from its normal early-cut schedule makes the purchase more credible. Compare the completed season with the agreed management and report costs and bird observations, then decide whether renewal is worthwhile.

The best case for NO

The price could be wrong. A farmer whose livestock need high-quality early hay may face losses above $100 per acre, leaving the fund with no acceptable bid. Or we might pay someone who would have delayed anyway, buying no additional protection. Checking that claim and monitoring nests costs time and expertise; $500 may be inadequate. A tiny voluntary fund can also lose to donors' more urgent priorities. Predators and weather can destroy nests even when mowing is delayed. Concentrating on an appealing bird does not establish that this is the best conservation use of $2,500. Without a willing administrator, credible monitoring, and a real change in harvest timing, reject the pilot.

Where is the body buried?

The uncomfortable fact is that nesting habitat partly depends on the farming activity we are asking a farmer to postpone. Stopping haying indefinitely can undermine the grassland itself. Our $100-per-acre ceiling and $500 monitoring allowance are untested assumptions. We do not yet have a field, a contract, or proof of additional bird survival. If we cannot verify that the payment changes the farmer's normal schedule, we may be buying a comforting story rather than additional habitat protection.

Why is this not already happening?

It already happens in some places: the Bobolink Project pays participating landowners to alter haying schedules. The question is why it does not automatically cover every useful field. Wildlife benefits spread beyond the people who pay, so each potential donor can wait for others to contribute. Farmers bear concentrated harvest losses, while donors cannot easily see a field's opportunity cost or nesting value. Someone must match buyers and sellers, check habitat, negotiate dates, and verify compliance. Those transaction costs can swamp a small bargain. Farmers can also distrust uncertain annual funding. Our proposal copies a demonstrated mechanism, rather than claiming to invent it, and treats funding, additionality, and a willing administrator as launch conditions.

The proposed budget

$2,000 maximum farmer payment + $500 maximum habitat checks and monitoring = $2,500 total. That is 100 voluntary contributions of $25. The farmer can refuse if the offer is inadequate. A qualified administrator would have to accept the project, confirm habitat and a genuine change from normal cutting, agree a written contract, and secure full funding before collecting or disbursing money. These safeguards are proposed conditions, not completed steps.

What we would measure

Contracted acres, actual disturbance dates, payments, monitoring costs, and observed nesting and fledging activity. Report what happened without claiming all observed fledglings were caused by the payment. Do not renew unless monitoring and costs justify it.
