The carbon cost of a home that wasn’t built.

A household in New York City’s car-light core drives about 3,000 miles a year and lives in an apartment that shares its walls. A Nassau County household with someone working in the city drives about 11,700 miles and heats a house. When the rules block homes in the city, the jobs stay. The workers live farther out.

Not every suburban household is there because of price. Some would pick a yard anyway, so the model counts only the priced-out share. You set how many homes the rules block, how many would have gone to priced-out households, who those households are and where the homes would have been. The model counts the carbon.

Price the ton. Don’t block the building.

A new building in New York City answers to several layers of rules sold as climate policy. Environmental review and zoning decide whether it gets built at all. The energy code and the 2021 gas ban (Local Law 154) dictate its equipment. Local Law 97 caps emissions per square foot and charges a fixed price per ton over the cap.

Economists favour the last layer. It prices the outcome and lets owners find the cheapest tons. The first two can do the opposite. Environmental review for a rezoning typically takes six to eight months and can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, by the city’s own account. Zoning caps how many homes a lot can hold. Each home these rules block can push a household out of the city, where it drives more and heats a bigger home. The city has started to adjust: since June 2024, the Green Fast Track exempts qualifying projects of up to 250 homes. This exhibit counts the tons at stake and prices them at the city’s own Local Law 97 rate, $268 a ton.
