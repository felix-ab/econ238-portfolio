A home in the city is a climate policy.

A household in the city’s car-light core drives about 3,100 miles a year and lives in an apartment that shares its walls. A household in Nassau County with someone working in the city drives about 12,000 miles and heats a house. When New York doesn’t build, the jobs stay and the workers live farther out.

Some of those commuters would choose a yard anyway. This model doesn’t assume they all want to move. You choose how many homes get built, who fills them, and where they go, and it counts what changes.

Regulate the ton, not the bulb.

A new building in New York answers to several layers of rules aimed at the climate. Zoning and environmental review decide whether it gets built at all. The energy code and the 2021 gas ban (Local Law 154) dictate equipment. Local Law 97 caps emissions per square foot and charges a fixed price per ton over the cap.

Economists favour the last layer: it prices the outcome and lets builders find the cheapest tons. The first layers can do the opposite. Each home they stop pushes a household outside the city, and that household emits more. This exhibit counts those tons, priced at the city’s own Local Law 97 rate.
